A shared file system works when an authorized colleague can predict where a document belongs, identify its status, and find the accountable owner without asking the person who created it. The goal is not a perfect hierarchy. It is a maintainable operating agreement for storing, naming, protecting, archiving, and disposing of business information.

The National Archives and Records Administration publishes [file and folder naming guidance](https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/bulletins/2015/2015-04-appendix-b.html) that emphasizes descriptive, consistent, meaningful names and platform-independent conventions. Its [file plan template](https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/scheduling/file-plan-template) illustrates recording a series or system, description, responsible office, and disposition authority. Those sources govern federal records contexts. The approach below borrows the durable organizational concepts but does not determine a private business’s legal retention duties.

## Inventory the work before drawing folders

Choose one bounded area such as client delivery, finance operations, people operations, or marketing. Record the information types it creates and receives, who uses them, the authoritative copy, sensitivity, current locations, active period, and required retention or deletion decision.

Look beyond ordinary documents. Include spreadsheets, images, signed records, exports, templates, meeting decisions, source files, email attachments that became operational records, and files generated by SaaS products. Mark duplicates and material that belongs in a specialist system such as accounting, customer management, or source control rather than a general drive.

Identify the business function each set supports. Functions change less often than employee names or temporary initiatives. A top-level folder called `Finance` is more durable than one called `Alex`, and `Client delivery` is clearer than `Misc projects`.

## Write a small file plan

For each information set, record:

- plain-language name and purpose;
- business owner and storage administrator;
- approved storage location;
- who may view, edit, share, or delete;
- naming rule and required metadata;
- active, closed, and archived state;
- retention or deletion authority; and
- backup or recovery expectation.

Keep this plan short enough to use. It is the map between business activity and storage, not a catalog of every file. If retention depends on law, regulation, contract, tax, employment, litigation, or professional obligations, have the appropriate specialist set the rule. Do not invent a period because a folder template needs a number.

## Build a shallow, predictable structure

Use top levels for durable functions, then a small number of consistent sublevels. For example:

```text
Client-delivery/
  Active/
    Client-code_Project-name/
      01_Admin/
      02_Working/
      03_Deliverables/
  Closed/
```

The numbering is optional; consistency is the value. Avoid deep nesting that creates long paths and makes sharing boundaries difficult to understand. Do not mix an access-controlled confidential folder under a broadly shared tree unless the platform’s permission behavior is tested and clear.

Separate active, reference, template, and closed material. A template should have a controlled master rather than many near-identical copies. Closed work should become read-only or otherwise protected according to the business process instead of remaining indefinitely editable beside active work.

## Adopt one naming convention

Choose only elements that help people identify and sort a file. A pattern might be:

```text
2026-08-21_client-code_document-purpose_v01.ext
```

Define the date format, approved abbreviations, separator, version style, and status terms. Use names that remain meaningful if a file leaves its folder. Keep characters compatible with the systems used by the business, and avoid changing a file extension to make a format appear different.

Do not use `final`, `final2`, and `really-final` as a version system. If the platform has reliable version history and one shared document, prefer that history. When separate versioned files are required, increment them consistently and identify the approved issue through a controlled status or location.

Names should not expose unnecessary sensitive data. A medical, disciplinary, financial, or credential-related detail may be inappropriate in a filename visible through search or notifications even when the file itself is restricted.

## Design permissions from roles

Create access groups around job functions rather than granting every file to individuals one at a time. Apply least privilege: people should have the access required for assigned work, and no broader. Separate routine contributors from administrators who can change sharing, retention, or deletion controls.

Test the permission model with representative accounts. Verify internal users, contractors, guests, link sharing, inherited permissions, and access after moving a folder. Determine whether a restricted child folder can accidentally inherit or expose access through its parent.

Give every top-level area a business owner who approves access and a storage administrator who implements it. Schedule reviews and remove access promptly when roles or contracts end. Avoid shared credentials.

## Migrate in controlled waves

Do not drag every historical folder into a new tree in one operation. Select an active area, freeze a copy window, remove obvious duplicates only with owner approval, map old locations to new locations, and preserve required metadata. Keep a migration log for important omissions or exceptions.

Test links, integrations, shortcuts, shared references, and permissions after moving. Communicate the cutover and make the old location read-only for a defined transition where practical. If both locations stay editable, conflicting authoritative copies will reappear.

Provide examples for common filing decisions. Ask users to file real work during the pilot and note where the structure is ambiguous. Revise the plan rather than creating one-off folders for every question.

## Maintain the system

Review active folders, orphaned owners, broad links, empty structures, duplicates, and closed work on a defined cadence. Make new-project setup use a controlled template. Add storage decisions to employee and contractor onboarding and offboarding.

Track practical measures: search or retrieval failures, orphaned areas, broad-sharing exceptions, projects with no owner, and items waiting for archive or deletion decisions. Storage volume alone does not measure organization quality.

Back up information according to its recovery need, but do not confuse backup with filing or retention. A backup copy does not make the live structure understandable, and deleting a live file may not remove every retained backup immediately.

## Limits and governance boundary

This method does not determine whether an item is a legal record, how long it must be retained, where regulated information may be stored, or when litigation holds apply. National Archives guidance is not a private-sector retention schedule. Qualified legal, privacy, security, tax, HR, and industry advice may be required.

The useful deliverable is a small file plan, predictable hierarchy, naming guide, permission matrix, migration record, and named maintenance owner. A shared drive becomes dependable when its rules survive the departure of the person who first organized it.
